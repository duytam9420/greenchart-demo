Calculating Portfolio Variance

Moving closer to our goal of measuring a portfolio's risk (variance), in this article we look at the correlation coefficient and the correlation matrix. In earlier articles, we learned how to calculate the variance–covariance matrix, although on its own it does not mean much. It is, however, one of the key inputs we use in this article. 

What is the correlation coefficient, and what does it mean?

The correlation coefficient is a statistic that measures the strength of the relationship between two variables. Its value lies between -1 and 1. A negative correlation means the two variables move in opposite directions (perfectly inversely at -1); conversely, the two variables are positively correlated when the coefficient is positive (perfectly together at 1).

So the closer the absolute value of the correlation coefficient is to 1, the stronger the relationship between the two variables. When it equals 0, the two variables are completely independent of each other.

The formula above is easy to apply with two stocks. But how is correlation determined for a five-stock portfolio? Here we need to use matrix operations.

In that case, the portfolio's correlations are expressed as a correlation matrix between the stocks, of size n × n. So for the five-stock portfolio from the previous chapter, we need a 5 × 5 matrix.

Based on the formula and the covariances already calculated in the matrix above, we now need the denominator: the product of the standard deviations of the stocks in the portfolio. 

To do this, we first calculate the standard deviation of each individual stock. This is fairly simple, since the square root of the variance is the standard deviation. Just use Excel's "STDEV()" function on a stock's daily return series, and the result is the standard deviation of that stock's returns.

The step above only gives us each stock's own standard deviation; what we need now is the product of standard deviations for each pair of stocks in the portfolio. Portfolio variance

Having calculated the portfolio's correlation matrix in the previous section, we now analyse the portfolio's risk by calculating its variance.

First, we need the weight of each stock in the portfolio – how much money you put into each of the five stocks above. Suppose that with $1,000 of available capital, I allocate the portfolio as follows:

  • VPB $100 (10%)
  • HPG $200 (20%)
  • VHM $400 (40%)
  • VNM $200 (20%)
  • FPT $100 (10%)

The next step is to calculate the weighted standard deviation. The weighted standard deviation is simply a stock's weight multiplied by its standard deviation. For example, VPB's standard deviation is 2.36%, so its weighted standard deviation is 10% × 2.36% = 0.236%

*Note: the weights of the stocks in a portfolio must add up to 100%

We now have everything we need to calculate the "portfolio variance". The formula is:

Portfolio variance = Square root (transpose of the weighted standard deviations × correlation matrix × weighted standard deviations)

Step 3. Take the square root of this number to get the portfolio variance

The result is 1.57%, the portfolio variance of the five-stock portfolio we were looking for.
