The Risk : Reward Ratio

Green Chart15 September 2022Updated 3 July 20264 min read

As a new trader, you have probably heard people talk about the risk : reward (R:R) ratio more than once, with advice such as: "you need a good R:R ratio above 1" or "professional traders usually have a minimum R:R ratio of 1:2"… But that is only one side of the story.

You can take trades with a 1:2 risk : reward ratio and still be a consistently losing trader (and I will prove it to you shortly). Likewise, you can take trades with a risk : reward ratio below 1 and still be consistently profitable. Why? In this article, I will give you the full picture so you understand how to use the risk : reward ratio properly. Here is what we will discuss:

  • What is the risk : reward ratio, really?
  • The secret to finding your edge (hint: the risk : reward ratio alone is not enough)
  • How to set a proper stop-loss and define your risk limit.

What is the risk : reward ratio, really?

The risk : reward ratio measures how much you can potentially earn for every dollar you risk. For example:

If you have a risk : reward ratio of 1:3, you are risking $1 to potentially make $3.

If you have a risk : reward ratio of 1:5, you are risking $1 to potentially make $5. You see what I mean? Now here is the biggest lie you have ever heard about the risk : reward ratio.

"You need a risk : reward ratio of at least 1:2." Why is that a lie? Because the risk : reward ratio on its own is meaningless. Here is an example:

Suppose you have a risk : reward ratio of 1:2 (for every winning trade, you make $2). But your win rate is 20%. So out of 10 trades, you have 8 losers and 2 winners.

Let's do the maths…

Total losses = $1 × 8 = –$8

Total gains = $2 × 2 = $4

Net loss = –$4

Now I hope you understand that the risk : reward ratio on its own is a meaningless measure. Instead, you must combine your risk : reward ratio with your win rate to know whether you will make money in the long run (this is called your trading expectancy).

The secret to finding your edge (hint: the risk : reward ratio alone is not enough)

Here it is…

E = [ 1 + ( W / L ) ] x P – 1

Where:

W is your average win

L is your average loss

P is your win rate

Here is an example: you take 10 trades. Six win and four lose. That means your win rate is 6/10, or 60%.

If your six winning trades make you $3,000, your average win is $3,000 / 6 = $500.

If your four losing trades cost you $1,600, your average loss is $1,600 / 4 = $400.

Next, plug these numbers into the expectancy formula:

E = [ 1 + ( 500 / 400 ) ] x 0.6 – 1 = 0.35, or 35%.

In this example, your trading strategy's expectancy is 35% (a positive expectancy).

This means your trading strategy will make $0.35 for every dollar you trade over the long run. So here is the truth:

There is no such thing as "a minimum risk : reward ratio of 1:2".

Because you can have a risk : reward ratio of 1:0.5 and, if your win rate is high enough… you will still be profitable in the long run. The most important metric in trading is not the risk : reward ratio or the win rate. It is your trading expectancy.

Your trading expectancy

You can calculate your own trading expectancy by keeping a trading journal over a long enough period – at least three to six months. You can then calculate your win rate and your average profit/loss per trade. Keep an eye on your trading expectancy: whether it improves or declines will show you whether your trading is getting better. 

Happy Trading !
