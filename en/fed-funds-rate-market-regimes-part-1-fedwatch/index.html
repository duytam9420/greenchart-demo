[TECHNICAL INSIGHTS] – The FFR and Its Impact on Market Regimes (Part 1)

In this post I will try to analyse how the FedWatch Tool calculates probabilities for FFR targets and how they affect market expectations; I will also use other variables for a fuller assessment.

Objectives of this post:

    • Get a basic understanding of the FFR.

    • Briefly understand how the FedWatch Tool calculates probabilities and build a similar monitoring tool.

    • Understand the relationships between the variables and factors.

    • From these relationships, find regimes based on how this variable affects markets such as the S&P 500 and the VN-Index, to generate trade ideas.

Conclusions of Part 1:

Thanks for reading! Subscribe for free to receive new posts and support my work.

    • Changes in the FFR (distance) directly affect oil (OIL) returns, and this is a causal relationship that also runs "the other way round" (=]]). In other words, a move in one affects the other and vice versa (both watch each other to move).

    • Likewise, the VN-Index has a causal relationship with oil prices, and vice versa.

    • Moves in the SPX affect the VN-Index and affect gold prices (GOLD). So if you trade gold, watch the SPX; likewise, if you trade the VN-Index, watch the SPX too. I also cannot hold both at the same time, because these two have an "extramarital" relationship with the SPX (=]]).

    • The SPX's Beta to the VN-Index is positive more than 80% of the time, and its R2 also covers the largest area among the exogenous factors. Combined with the results above, it is one of the most important explanatory factors with a big influence on VN-Index returns.

Notes:

    • The data is accessed and calculated from the vnstocks library (http://vnstocks.com) and yfinance.

    • The data for all variables covers 2014 to date.

FED FUND RATES

In theory, the Fed Funds Rate (FFR) is like the benchmark interest rate from which the rates or yields of other assets are determined. So what is the FFR? Why the FFR? How many kinds of FFR are there?

    • The FFR (EFFR) is the overnight rate between depository institutions (banks, financial institutions) within the Federal Reserve system, calculated as a volume-weighted median (details at this link: https://www.newyorkfed.org/markets/reference-rates/effr?). The FFR therefore reflects the most basic level of interest in the asset that is money (specifically the USD). To help investors, hedge funds and financial institutions hedge moves in line with their expectations, CME created derivatives (futures, options) on the FFR (or so that everyone can bet together, with CME as the bookmaker =]]).

    • Moves in the FFR are overseen by one party at the top, the Fed, which conducts monetary policy to meet its mandate of price stability and maximum employment (the "dual mandate"), and thereby helps keep long-term interest rates moderate. To steer rates, the Fed sets a "target range" and announces its stance at the FOMC, which meets eight times a year; four of these meetings (at the end of each quarter: March, June, September, December) publish projections with the dot plot. To keep rates within the "target range", the Fed (mainly) sets administered rates and actively manages the supply of reserves. For more details, see this link: https://www.newyorkfed.org/markets/domestic-market-operations/monetary-policy-implementation?

    • So the FFR essentially carries two elements – the market element and the Fed's intervention – which together reveal expectations about the value of the USD.

So to understand the USD from a monetary-policy perspective, I would have to read the FOMC minutes to grasp the Fed's direction, then follow the FFR from spot to derivatives, and also watch other macro factors for a complete assessment. That is quite a lot to follow, and honestly I think it is very hard to get from those observations to a real view of what the Fed is doing. CME (the bookmaker) offers a tool giving the probabilities of expected rate changes, the CME FedWatch Tool, so that everyone can follow along – or, as I like to call it, a betting score (=]], it's a bookmaker, so it must have odds). What I like about it is that it shows market sentiment, and in a way it is quite similar to the VIX (in my view, both are calculated from derivatives and reflect implied expectations). Assuming that the expected probabilities of rate changes can affect market states, I will dig into how it is calculated and test whether it can be used to predict or classify regimes.

    • So how is the probability calculated once we have the ratio delta/0.25 (0.25, or 25 bps):
        • If the absolute value of this ratio is < 1 —> use the probability formula above.

        • If the absolute value of this ratio is > 1, roughly speaking the expectation has moved beyond the 0–0.25 range into a larger range:
            • For example, if the value is 1.2, it moves into the 0.5–0.75 range, and we set P[Hike] = 0.2, i.e. 20% for a 75 bps hike, and P[No Hike] = 1 – 0.2 = 0.8, i.e. 80% for a 50 bps hike

            • If the value is 2.2, the threshold is 0.25 * (1 + 2) = 0.75, the new range is 0.75–1.00, and P[Hike] = 20% for a 100 bps hike and P[No Hike] = 80% for a 75 bps hike.

Very convoluted, but in the end the key ratio is still delta/0.25. I only need to calculate this to see how representative it is at a given time. However, there is a big problem: I do not have historical futures data (CME charges for it, and yfinance does not have complete data). The current yfinance data only has the latest futures price, meaning I have the market's expectations but no prices to compare against to calculate this probability.

REGIMES

a) Relationships between the factors

I see that most people care a lot about how the economic calendar affects the Vietnamese market, especially the FOMC and other macro events in the US, EU and elsewhere. However, I have some doubts about the theory versus the reality of how changes in these events affect markets (both the home country and Vietnam). Do they really have a significant effect? If so, how should we understand it? Does that understanding hold for Vietnam?…

There are many questions about this! If we put all these factors into a basic regression model, I would subjectively treat them as exogenous variables, since they may have an exogenous effect on what I am observing. OK! Let's see whether they really affect Vietnam:

(The FFR here is calculated from FFR futures on yfinance; the VN-Index has been converted into USD for consistency (so you won't see it break 1,500 =]]))

The chart above shows the VN-Index against other assets (the S&P 500, gold and oil) and the FFR, with Spearman correlations over a 252-day look-back window. I draw only a few preliminary observations:

    • The correlation between VN-Index and SPX returns seems to be the most consistently positive, with the highest share of time in positive correlation (almost 80% of the time), followed by oil prices (just over 40%) and, third, the FFR (quite interesting).

    • More specifically, looking at the 252-day rolling Beta between these variables and the VN-Index, the SPX and oil also co-move the most, with Beta > 0 about 79% and 67% of the time respectively (naturally, since Beta = correlation / std). R-squared (R2) (used to assess how much one variable explains another; e.g. 0.2 means 20% explained) also stays highest for the SPX and oil, although still fairly modest. Interestingly, at extremes (2020 and 2024), R-squared jumps sharply; my subjective view is that these extremes are crisis points where the whole market shares the same view (=]]).

    • Most of the time the FFR explains very little, but there are periods when it explains more, with R2 rising steadily – usually times when the other assets' correlations are fading.

Based on this, I will run a small test of a causal network: roughly, a Granger causality test of all the moves from these variables to the VN-Index. However, since this tests linear relationships, it must meet some statistical requirements for linear regression, one of which is a test of the stationarity of the time series.
