In the previous article, we adjusted the weights of a portfolio with a fixed variance (risk). Continuing the topic of portfolio optimisation, in this article we approach portfolio optimisation through the efficient frontier.

What is the efficient frontier? 

The efficient frontier (EF) is the curve representing the set of stock portfolios for which:

  • The portfolio's expected return is the highest for a fixed level of risk, or
  • The portfolio's risk is the lowest for a fixed level of expected return.

The efficient frontier is plotted as a scatter chart with two variables: return (y-axis), usually represented by the compound annual growth rate (CAGR), and risk (x-axis), usually represented by the portfolio's variance. 

So how is the efficient frontier determined, and why is it one of the tools that help us choose an efficient portfolio? Let's move on to the next section.

Steps to build the efficient frontier

So far, we have only optimised a portfolio by choosing the one with the lowest risk (variance). We also know that for any given level of risk, there are always portfolios with different returns. In this section, we dig deeper into that idea, again with the aim of finding the optimal portfolio.

Going back to our familiar five-stock portfolio – VPB, HPG, VHM, VNM and FPT – the portfolio's variance and expected return are 1.57% and 4.25% respectively. We will now raise the risk level to 2% and find the highest and lowest returns this portfolio can achieve.

Treating 1.57% as the lowest risk level, with a return of 4.25%, we will keep raising the portfolio's risk and finding the highest and lowest returns, paying attention to the weights as well. After doing this, we will have a certain number of observations.

Finally, we plot these observations on a scatter chart (at each risk level, we have one data point for the highest return and one for the lowest; successive risk levels give us a set of data points that, when joined, form an arc-like curve).

Note that you are free to adjust the portfolio's risk level for your own calculations; it depends entirely on your risk appetite. The 2% figure I use is just for the calculation, not a required number.

The chart above is a visual representation of the efficient frontier (EF). What does it tell us about portfolio optimisation, and why does it matter? 

First, the leftmost point represents the portfolio with the lowest risk – our original portfolio (variance 1.57% and expected return 4.25%).

Second, apart from the leftmost point, at each level of risk (x-axis) there are always two corresponding points, a high return and a low return (y-axis). The higher point shows the high return our portfolio can achieve in favourable conditions, and the lower point the opposite.

Put simply, once we have determined the level of risk that suits our investment appetite, we should aim for the highest return (the upper point).

More broadly, as investors we should only target portfolios on the upper part of the curve (the upper line when we join the data points), because they represent the best, most efficient portfolios.
