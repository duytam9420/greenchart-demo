A recap

In the previous article, we looked at variance and covariance in connection with the two common types of risk, systematic and unsystematic risk. Having understood the basic difference between these two types of risk, we now turn to portfolio risk, also known as portfolio variance.

Specifically, variance measures how far a stock's returns deviate from its own average return. Covariance measures how one stock's returns vary relative to another stock's returns. 

In the examples so far, the formulas have only been applied to a pair of stocks, whereas in practice a portfolio contains many more. To estimate a portfolio's variance, covariance and correlation, we therefore need the variance–covariance matrix.

In this chapter, we discuss how to estimate the variance–covariance of a portfolio using matrix multiplication and some related concepts. The resulting covariance matrix plays an important role in calculating the correlation matrix. This is the basis for calculating portfolio variance, and from there, how much risk you take on by holding a portfolio of stocks.

Along the way, we will also touch on "asset allocation" and how it affects portfolio return and risk. You will also get a quick introduction to "Value at Risk" (VaR).

Looking at risk in detail from a trader's perspective, this chapter will help you identify trading risk and ways to reduce it.

The variance–covariance matrix

Before we continue, we need to clarify the phrase "variance–covariance matrix". Does it mean one matrix containing both variance and covariance values, or two separate matrices, one of variances and one of covariances? 

The answer is: a single matrix containing both variance and covariance values. If there are five stocks, for example, the matrix shows each stock's variance and its covariance with the other four stocks. The following example will make this easier to picture. You will also need to know some basic matrix operations. If you don't, this Khan Academy video will help you understand matrix multiplication here.

A well-diversified portfolio usually contains around 10–15 stocks. However, visualising a matrix of this size in Excel would be very complicated, and many beginners might feel overwhelmed by its size. I have therefore decided to use a five-stock portfolio so that the calculation is both clear and manageable for beginners.

Suppose the five stocks in my portfolio are: VPB, HPG, VHM, VNM and FPT

The covariance matrix for a five-stock portfolio is 5 × 5. In general, if there are "k" stocks in the portfolio, the covariance matrix is k × k (k rows and k columns).

Specifically, we first calculate the n × k excess return matrix, then multiply its transpose by it. This matrix multiplication produces a k × k matrix. We then divide each element of this k × k matrix by the n observations. The result is the k × k covariance matrix.

Step 4 – Perform the matrix multiplication XT × X to create a k × k matrix

XT is the transpose of X, created by swapping the rows and columns of the original matrix X. Note that this operation produces a k × k matrix, where k is the number of stocks in the portfolio. In our case, the matrix is 5 × 5.

In the results table above, the cells marked in red show that the covariance of HPG with VHM is the same as the covariance of VHM with HPG – there is no difference (both are 0.04%). 

In addition, the blue cell shows that the covariance of VPB with itself is 0.04%. What does this value tell us? Simply that the covariance of a stock with itself equals its variance.

That is why this matrix is called the variance–covariance matrix: it contains both values.

You now know how to calculate the variance–covariance matrix. On its own, however, this matrix does not tell us much. What we really need is the "correlation matrix" – the key piece and the destination for estimating portfolio variance/risk. We will discuss it in the next chapter!
